Detection methodology

A trajectory, not a signature.

GHAST doesn't match against a list of known jamming signatures. It predicts where a vessel should physically be next, and treats a large enough gap between that prediction and what's reported as the signal worth investigating.

Next-state prediction

Predicted position vs. reported position

Given a vessel's recent track — position, speed over ground, course, heading, rate of turn — a sequence model predicts its next plausible state. Reported positions normally track that prediction closely, bounded by the vessel's real physical limits. A deviation that exceeds those limits is the anomaly score.

Fleet-wide check

One vessel, or a whole area?

A flagged deviation on its own doesn't say why. A clustering pass across every vessel currently in the area does: if one vessel is affected while its neighbors track normally, that points to targeted spoofing. If many vessels in the same area flag at once, that points to area jamming instead — a materially different situation for an analyst to walk into.

Why this works without insurer data

Three public data sources, no proprietary access required

01
Live public AIS feeds
Real, continuously updating vessel tracks from public terrestrial and satellite AIS sources — no vessel or insurer relationship needed to reach a working system.
02
Public research datasets
Labeled synthetic and real-vs-simulated spoofed-track studies (IEEE DataPort and published literature) ground the model against credible published-level performance.
03
A self-generated incident history
Every investigated anomaly feeds back into the jamming-zone map and incident history — a dataset that only exists because the system has been running.
